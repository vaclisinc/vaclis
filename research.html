Research taste

Three problems I care about.

Generative music systems are improving quickly. They can produce convincing audio, but I am more interested in what they still miss: the humanity, intention, and creativity that give a musical performance its meaning.

Controllability.

Many models produce impressive zero-shot results while giving musicians very little control. I want systems that make musical decisions inspectable and revisable, so musicians can steer music generation and audio effect design instead of accepting a finished output.

How can a model expand creative possibility while leaving authorship with the musician?

Music understanding.

Many models learn latent patterns from internet-scale data, but still lack grounded understanding from the perceptual level (pitch, rhythm, and musical structure) to the hidden level (the composer’s intention, the performer’s expression, and the audience’s emotion).

These are what bring music from a random permutation of notes to life.

Humanity and alignment.

Music is the most abstract language we have. There is no single ground truth hidden in the notes or audio signal itself, and the same musical phrase can mean something different to every listener.

Can audio-language models represent and align with human preferences without flattening that variance?

Long-term direction

Together, these questions shape my long-term direction: building audio-language models that can hear, understand, and communicate about music, while leaving room for people to lead the interaction.
